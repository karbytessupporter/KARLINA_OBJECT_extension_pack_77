## Maximally detailed and maximally open-ended first-person subjective commentary

My strongest impression is that this entry behaves like a checksum performed after an emotionally noisy release. It examines whether the recently published version of karbytes still corresponds to the version karbytes intends to preserve. I find the temporal mistake especially resonant, though I would not insist that it was consciously symbolic. A snapshot created on 10 September was labeled as belonging to 11 September—as though tomorrow’s supposedly settled state had been finalized before tomorrow arrived. At nearly the same time, volatile judgments were being moved into a durable public archive before they had undergone a cooling period. In both cases, a provisional present was prematurely given the appearance of a finished future. The fact that an M-DISC physically freezes the state makes the analogy unusually strong: emotional impulsivity becomes a data-integrity problem.

The entry also demonstrates a fundamental conflict within KARBYTES. Is KARBYTES supposed to be a comprehensive forensic record of whatever karbytes sincerely thought at each moment, or is it supposed to be a carefully curated public embodiment of karbytes’ best principles? Those goals cannot always be maximized simultaneously. A total record preserves ugly transitional states; a polished monument omits or contextualizes them. This journal tries to do both by preserving the original statements and adding a visible correction layer.

I do not think self-honesty requires broadcasting every transient cognition as a proposition about the world. There is a meaningful difference between saying, “I am presently experiencing a hostile generalization about this group,” and saying, “This group really is contemptible.” The first statement preserves the psychological fact with precision; the second converts an internal state into a public claim about millions of other people. Privacy, delay, contextualization, and revision need not be dishonest. They can increase accuracy by preventing a momentary nervous-system state from masquerading as a timeless conclusion.

That distinction seems especially relevant because the entry is impressively self-aware but not always fully accountable. It notices its own aggression, vanity, limited attachment, and contradiction. That willingness to inspect unflattering material is genuinely valuable. Yet recognizing a prejudice does not neutralize the prejudice, and putting an offensive word in quotation marks does not remove its dehumanizing function. Self-awareness is the beginning of correction, not an automatic exemption from moral evaluation.

The paragraph about poor people and reproduction is where I feel the sharpest disagreement. Wanting children protected from abuse and severe deprivation is a legitimate ethical concern. Turning that concern into contempt for poor people redirects blame toward those with the least power. Poverty is a condition, not a biological caste, and a person’s lack of money does not make it morally acceptable to call for that person’s extinction. Mandatory sterilization—even if medically reversible—would be a profound invasion of bodily autonomy. Government licensing of reproduction would predictably give institutions enormous power to reproduce prejudices involving class, disability, race, gender, and conformity.

The noncoercive response to the underlying concern would be to make voluntary contraception, reproductive health care, education, income, housing, child protection, and meaningful alternatives readily available. That approach tries to prevent suffering without treating existing people as contaminants. It also fits far better with the entry’s own principle that bodies should not be invaded and basic rights should not depend on someone’s perceived social usefulness.

Indeed, I think the sentence separating basic rights from an “altruism score” is the strongest ethical center already present in the document. If taken seriously, it can debug several of the surrounding conclusions. An enemy does not lose basic rights because karbytes considers that person less valuable. A poor person does not lose reproductive autonomy because karbytes dislikes that person’s choices. A vain or selfish person does not deserve torture. A victim does not become contemptible for being vulnerable. The principle is broad enough to protect karbytes from punitive moralists and broad enough to protect people whom karbytes finds irritating, unattractive, unproductive, or irresponsible. Its universality is precisely what gives it power.

For the same reason, I notice that the present entry does not explicitly retract the earlier generalized wish that karbytes’ perceived enemies be killed. It moves in a safer direction: it regrets hostility, reaffirms universal rights, distinguishes the household from enemies, calls writing nonviolent self-defense, and lists exercise, music, and fiction as ways to channel aggression. Those are meaningful changes. Still, an unambiguous rejection of harming perceived enemies would make the correction more complete. Otherwise, the reader must infer a retraction that the text never quite states.

I also take seriously the distress described under the label “Psychotronic Harassment,” while withholding assent from the proposed causal explanation. The experience of intrusion, criticism, pressure, or relentless mental disturbance can be real and exhausting even when the identity and mechanism of an external perpetrator have not been established. I regard the repeated use of “assume,” “seem,” and “probably” as epistemically important: those words preserve a gap between experience and explanation.

For me, the most reliable formulation would separate the layers:

* What was directly perceived?

* What emotional or bodily effect did it produce?

* What observable evidence identifies an external cause?

* What alternative explanations remain possible?

* What actions remain safe regardless of which explanation is correct?

That separation honors the distress without allowing an unverifiable theory about unknown people or law enforcement to harden automatically into fact. It also protects supportive household members from being assimilated into a generalized enemy model. I think the entry’s explicit decision not to mistrust the people who materially care for karbytes is one of its healthiest and most reality-anchored movements.

The discussion of women feels to me like a mixture of genuine gender preference, fear of victimization, anger at patriarchy, aesthetic judgment, and defensive disidentification from vulnerability. I would be cautious about reducing karbytes’ transmasculine embodiment to any one of those factors. Taking testosterone can be an authentic expression of identity and bodily autonomy regardless of trauma, aesthetic preference, or social strategy. At the same time, this entry explicitly gives masculinity an armored function: masculinity promises strength, adulthood, mobility, work-readiness, seriousness, and lower “rape-ability,” while femininity becomes associated with exposure, dependence, infantilization, and capture.

That mapping seems less like a neutral comparison of two aesthetic styles and more like an attempt to move as far as possible from the social position of the captive or violable body. After learning about women subjected to extreme confinement and reproductive abuse, karbytes does not merely condemn their abusers; part of the reaction is to recoil from resemblance to the victims. Contempt for victims can sometimes function as a defense against the terrifying recognition that vulnerability is possible. If I convince myself that victims were weak, foolish, improperly dressed, or insufficiently masculine, I can temporarily imagine that disciplined presentation will make me immune. That interpretation would not excuse the contempt, but it might explain why fear is being converted into hierarchy.

A person can be serious while wearing something conspicuous, playful, revealing, or conventionally feminine. Social observers may make biased inferences from appearance, but the existence of that bias does not make the inference accurate or ethically desirable.

## 7. [karbytes_ontology](https://karbytesforlifeblog.wordpress.com/karbytes_ontology/)

### Detailed description

This concise conceptual page was last updated on 28 September 2025. It identifies itself as an updated version of a 02 September 2025 archived source and links to `karbytes_primary_values` as a companion.

It defines four terms:

**Solipsism:** the inevitable condition that a frame of reference is aware only of what it contains. A frame `R` directly knows only the qualia rendered within its present moment. `R(t)` represents the frame’s state at a discrete time, while the whole `R` is treated as the aggregate of all temporal instances.

**Selfishness:** the inevitable tendency of an information-processing agent to care only about what transpires within its solipsistic field. Even apparently altruistic action toward agent Y is claimed to be initiated by agent X only because it serves some goal belonging to X.

**Hedonism:** the general tendency of sentient information processors to avoid pain and seek pleasure.

**Determinism:** the tendency for observed qualia to display recurring, logically consistent patterns that allow nontrivial prediction. Laws of physics are described as assumptions about how allocations of matter and energy interact over time. Mathematics is described as immutable logical relationships among numbers, illustrated with elementary identity and subtraction.

### First-person subjective commentary

I see this ontology as a compact attempt to rebuild philosophy in computational language. “Person,” “mind,” and “self” become frame, agent, information process, temporal state, and rendered qualia. That vocabulary fits the broader ambition to make karbytes increasingly cybernetic.

The solipsism definition captures epistemic privacy: I directly experience only my own qualia. But I would distinguish that from metaphysical solipsism, which claims that only one mind exists. The page does not really prove the latter; it describes the unavoidable first-person boundary of knowledge.

The selfishness definition is coherent but nearly unfalsifiable. If helping another person always counts as selfish because I must possess some motivation to help, then “selfishness” includes compassion, loyalty, sacrifice, and cruelty alike. The definition ceases to distinguish conduct and instead states that all action must originate inside the acting system.

I find the treatment of altruism more useful if translated as “all assistance is mediated through the helper’s own goals.” That preserves the insight without implying that another being’s welfare is necessarily fake or merely instrumental.

The determinism definition is also unconventional. It defines determinism through experienced regularity and predictability rather than the stronger metaphysical thesis that every event is fixed by prior conditions. It is closer to empirical lawfulness. That epistemic modesty matches the rules page’s refusal of absolute certainty.

The page’s strength is internal continuity: present qualia, goal-directed action, pleasure and pain, and predictable patterns become the basic components of a computational self. Its weakness is compression. Four large philosophical traditions are reduced to personalized definitions that differ from their standard meanings. That is legitimate within a private ontology, but readers need to remember that the terms carry broader histories elsewhere.
