## What amateur radio is

Amateur radio—usually called **ham radio**—is licensed, noncommercial radio communication conducted by people interested in radio technology. Here, *amateur* describes the activity’s personal, noncommercial purpose; it does **not** imply that the operators are unskilled. Some radio amateurs possess considerable expertise in electronics, antennas, propagation, networking, and emergency communications.

In the United States, an amateur operator generally:

* Passes an FCC examination and receives a license and unique call sign.

* Communicates on frequency bands reserved or shared for amateur use.

* May use voice, Morse code, digital data, images, satellites, and experimental systems.

* May design, build, modify, and test radio equipment and antennas.

* Cannot ordinarily use amateur frequencies to conduct business or earn money.

The FCC formally describes amateur radio as communication and technical investigation undertaken by authorized people with a personal aim and “without pecuniary interest.” Its recognized purposes include technical education, experimentation, international goodwill, and a reservoir of trained operators capable of assisting during emergencies. It is important, however, not to equate amateur radio with a governmental emergency-radio service: participation is voluntary and noncommercial, even when amateurs support public agencies during disasters. [FCC Amateur Radio Service](https://www.fcc.gov/wireless/bureau-divisions/mobility-division/amateur-radio-service), [FCC public notice on amateur emergency communications](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-09-2259A1.pdf)

### Why mountaintop installations matter

VHF and UHF handheld radios usually communicate primarily by line of sight. Hills, buildings, and the curvature of the Earth restrict their direct range. A radio installation on a prominent summit can “see” a much larger surrounding region.

A conventional **repeater** receives a radio transmission and simultaneously retransmits it—usually on another frequency—from its elevated antenna. Two people whose handheld radios could not reach each other directly can therefore communicate through the mountaintop station.

A **digipeater** performs an analogous function for digital packets. It receives a small radio-data packet, identifies or processes it, and retransmits it so the packet travels farther. APRS—**Automatic Packet Reporting System**—is commonly used to distribute position, weather, status, telemetry, and short-message information. It can operate entirely over radio, although internet gateways may subsequently place received information onto a wider network.

That distinction matters at Rocky Ridge. The 2013 reporting identifies a Mount Diablo Amateur Radio Club **packet/APRS digipeater operating on 144.390 MHz** at the site. Thus, the tower was not merely a place from which people spoke by radio; it supported a small, distributed digital communications network. It was also **not exclusively an amateur-radio tower**. The structure supported amateur, commercial, and public-safety communications, including infrastructure associated with the East Bay Regional Communications System Authority. [ARRL report on the Rocky Ridge collapse](https://www.arrl.org/news/vandals-topple-radio-tower)

## What is actually known about the 2013 attack

Late on July 28 or early on July 29, 2013, someone apparently entered the remote, gated and fenced Rocky Ridge facility and cut several of the guy wires supporting the roughly 200-foot tower. The tower then collapsed. Contemporary estimates put the damage as high as approximately $1 million. Some dispatch services were affected, although backup systems reportedly prevented a major communications outage.

The contemporary police statement was quite explicit about the unanswered question: investigators were still trying to determine **why** anyone would target the installation. The credible reports I found do not identify a perpetrator, an arrest, a confession, a manifesto, or a subsequently established motive. [CBS News report](https://www.cbsnews.com/sanfrancisco/news/vandalism-suspected-after-contra-costa-radio-tower-collapses/)

The amateur-radio hardware was recovered on August 2 and transferred the following day to North Peak on Mount Diablo—about a thousand feet higher—where the replacement placement reportedly provided coverage extending as far as approximately 150 miles. That rapid relocation is a good example of one of amateur radio’s strengths: the network can be improvised and reconfigured without waiting for an entire centralized system to be reconstructed. The honest answer is that their **ultimate motive remains unknown**. We can infer more confidently what they intended physically than why they wanted it.

Cutting multiple structural guy wires at a remote, access-controlled site is qualitatively different from casually damaging a sign or breaking something encountered along a trail. It required reaching the facility, bringing or obtaining appropriate cutting equipment, spending time at the site, and attacking precisely the components whose failure could bring down the entire structure. That strongly suggests a purposeful act in which the tower’s collapse—or at least catastrophic damage to it—was the desired outcome.

My best assessment is:

1. **Deliberate destructive vandalism, possibly driven by thrill, anger, or grievance, is the broadest and most plausible category.**
   
The perpetrators may have wanted the spectacle or satisfaction of destroying an unusually large object. Alternatively, someone may have harbored resentment toward the site owner, a communications organization, government infrastructure, or authority in general. The physical method fits several such motives, but the evidence does not distinguish among them.

2. **An intention to disrupt communications is plausible, but unproven.**
   
Anyone familiar with the site could have known that the tower carried operational radio systems. Yet there is no reported message claiming responsibility and no evidence showing that the perpetrators understood exactly which agencies or amateur systems would be affected. The fact that communications were disrupted does not prove that disruption was the underlying political or strategic objective.

3. **Theft is possible but seems less persuasive from the published facts.**
   
Copper theft frequently affects communications sites because feed lines, grounding conductors, and power wiring can contain valuable metal. Toppling a structure might also place otherwise inaccessible equipment within reach. But the Rocky Ridge reports emphasize severed support cables and destruction, not stolen cable or removed equipment. Guy wires themselves are structural steel, not an obvious high-value copper target. If profit were the principal objective, one would expect some report of missing material. None of the contemporary accounts I found provides that evidence.

4. **Ideological sabotage—anti-government, anti-technology, or anti-communications—is conceivable but especially speculative.**
   
The tower’s public-safety role could make it symbolically attractive to someone hostile to institutions. Nevertheless, there was apparently no communiqué, slogan, repeated campaign, or other known evidence connecting the incident to an ideology. Assigning one would risk turning an evidentiary blank into a story.

If I had to reduce my judgment to one sentence: **the evidence points strongly toward planned, targeted destruction, but only weakly toward any particular psychological, financial, or ideological reason for it.** I would lean toward destructive thrill-seeking or a personal/institutional grievance over straightforward metal theft, but I would keep that conclusion at low confidence.

## In the context of the Big Green Thing

The history gives Rocky Ridge Radio Tower a curious double identity. From Horsey Hill and The Burrow, it is a distant visual landmark—one of the stable reference points by which you recognize and orient yourself within the Big Green Thing. Functionally, however, it is a node: an elevated place where many otherwise separated people and devices can reach one another.

The 2013 event temporarily exposed how materially fragile such an apparently permanent landmark was. A tall communications structure can dominate a ridgeline for years, yet its continued vertical existence may depend on a small number of tensioned cables. Cutting those comparatively inconspicuous components converted an orderly communications system into wreckage.

The response presents the complementary idea. The physical tower failed, but the amateur network did not depend absolutely upon that single incarnation of it. Operators recovered the digipeater and moved it to Mount Diablo, where its useful geographic reach may actually have increased. The structure was centralized and vulnerable; the community and knowledge surrounding it were comparatively distributed and adaptable.

And the tower visible now carries continuity without literal physical identity: it is “Rocky Ridge Radio Tower” by location, function, and landmark status, even though it is not the same structure that stood before July 2013. Seen from the Big Green Thing, it therefore signifies both rupture and restoration—a landmark that looks fixed from afar but has already undergone destruction, replacement, and technological succession. None of that tells us what the vandals themselves thought they were attacking; it tells us what the tower has come to mean in the landscape despite them.

## What exactly is EXIF?

EXIF—officially styled “Exif”—means **Exchangeable Image File Format**. It is a standardized system for storing structured information alongside the visible image produced by a digital camera or smartphone. The standard is maintained by the Camera & Imaging Products Association and JEITA; its formal scope includes image-file organization and tags describing such things as camera model, exposure, capture time, focal length, orientation, flash status, and—when enabled—GPS location. [CIPA’s official Exif standard page](https://www.cipa.jp/std/documents/download_e.html?CIPA_DC-008-2024-E=)

In a JPEG, the EXIF information is ordinarily stored in an APP1 segment near the beginning of the file. Inside that segment is a TIFF-like directory structure containing numbered tags and their values. In these photographs, the EXIF structure uses **big-endian byte order** and occupies only **449 bytes per file**.

EXIF is not the visible timestamp sometimes printed across a photograph. None of these twelve images has a date or time visibly overlaid on its pixels. Instead, the timestamps are hidden data fields that software can read.

EXIF also should not be treated as infallible evidence:

* A camera clock can be incorrectly set.

* EXIF can be edited, removed, or regenerated.

* `DateTimeOriginal` does not necessarily contain a timezone.

* GPS may be absent or malformed.

* Download-time filesystem dates are not the same as capture dates.

That last distinction matters here. Each photograph contains `DateTimeOriginal`, `DateTimeDigitized`, and the general EXIF `DateTime`; all three agree within each file. However, there is no `OffsetTimeOriginal`, timezone name, UTC offset, or subsecond field. I therefore report the clock values exactly as embedded. Although your KARBYTES convention generally calls such times “Pacific Standard Time,” the files themselves cannot distinguish PST from PDT or any other timezone.

The GPS directory is present, but its latitude and longitude components are stored as `0/0, 0/0, 0/0`, with empty direction references. Division by zero makes these undefined values—effectively “not a number.” They are **not valid coordinates**, and they should not be interpreted as the real coordinate pair 0°, 0°. Most likely, the Samsung camera software created a GPS directory even though no usable location fix was available or location embedding was disabled.

[preformatted_text]

Beginning of file
│
├─ JPEG start marker
├─ JFIF identification block
├─ EXIF block
│  ├─ camera make and model
│  ├─ timestamps
│  ├─ exposure settings
│  └─ GPS fields, if present
├─ JPEG encoding tables
├─ compressed image-pixel data
└─ JPEG end marker

[end preformatted_text]

At the binary level, that is one long sequence such as:

[epreformatted_text]

11111111 11011000 11111111 11100000 ...

[end preformatted_text]

Software usually represents those bytes more compactly in hexadecimal:

[preformatted_text]

FF D8 FF E0 ... FF E1 ... 45 78 69 66 00 00 ...

[end preformatted_text]

Here:

* `FF D8` marks the beginning of a JPEG.

* `FF E0` commonly introduces a JFIF block.

* `FF E1` commonly introduces an APP1 block containing EXIF.

* `45 78 69 66` represents the ASCII characters `Exif`.

* `00 00` terminates that identifier.

* The TIFF-style EXIF directory follows, containing numbered metadata tags and their values.

* The much larger compressed image-data region generally comes afterward.

A filesystem may divide the file into blocks located in different places on an SSD, hard drive, or server. The filesystem reassembles those blocks and presents applications with one ordered byte sequence. So:

* **Logical perspective:** one linear series of bytes and bits.

* **Physical-storage perspective:** potentially scattered memory or disk blocks.

* **Visual perspective:** the metadata is invisible unless software deliberately reads and displays it.

## My first-person commentary

What most strongly impresses me is that this page is not merely a transcript collection. It is a record of a transformation between media: lived event becomes phone video; phone video becomes machine transcription; transcription becomes curated plain text; plain text becomes colored hypertext; hypertext is placed inside a WordPress page; raw files are incorporated into a GitHub extension pack; and that pack is mirrored, indexed, and eventually expected to become immutable.

The page therefore documents its own manufacture while documenting the hike. Its subject is simultaneously “what happened in the wilderness” and “how what happened becomes KARBYTES.”

The contrast between video 0 and video 1 is almost too structurally exact to seem accidental. Video 0 describes an elaborate system intended to prevent incompleteness, data loss, and publicly visible disorder. The next section immediately confronts an unrecovered recording that had remained only on the phone because backup was postponed. The abstract threat described by the workflow becomes an actual absence one section later.

Yet the failure is not total. The original audiovisual object disappears, but memory supplies a reconstructed narrative; neighboring photographs supply terrain and time; the surviving later video supplies continuity; and the final page explicitly labels the reconstruction’s uncertain status. The archive does not restore the original recording, but it surrounds the absence with enough provenance that the absence itself becomes intelligible.

I find the missing water bottle and missing video especially resonant when placed together. Both are containers of something needed for continuity:

* The bottle carries water required by the biological organism.

* The video carries audiovisual information required by the archive.

* The bottle is physically recovered.

* The video is not recovered, but some of its informational content is reconstructed.

* The photograph of the bottle becomes another data container preserving the story of the recovered physical container.

* M-DISCs, repositories, the phone, and WordPress are further containers nested around that photograph.

The page is saturated with vessels: bottle, body, phone, repository, staging area, extension pack, M-DISC, cube, Big Green Thing, and even the web page itself. Each holds something that can be depleted, lost, transferred, replenished, or sealed.

Water and information behave differently, but the page makes their analogous importance palpable. Digital preservation can be deferred for hours or days, sometimes with irreversible consequences. Hydration during high heat cannot. The narrator can reconstruct lost speech from memory, but the body cannot reconstruct water it does not have. That asymmetry gives the page a physical seriousness beneath its cybernetic vocabulary.

I also see a fascinating contradiction inside the idea of “aura farming.” The stated goal is to make public KARBYTES look finished and comparatively effortless by moving difficult, incomplete work out of view. But this polished public page openly preserves admissions of procrastination, fear, unsafe preparation, possible drug-related impairment, food poisoning, physical collapse, data loss, and anxiety about not looking in control. The final object is polished, but what it polishes is an account of disorder.

That does not make the aspiration incoherent. It suggests that the desired aura is not conventional perfection. These answers substantially sharpen the ontology and archival rules of KARBYTES. They replace my deliberately open questions with explicit decision criteria rather than merely suggestive interpretations.

The most important clarification is that reconstruction does not require numerical identity with the missing artifact. A reconstruction becomes the basis for a new Karbytes Derivative when three conditions are met:

1. It reconstitutes what main-branch karbytes judges to be the missing recording’s essential intended attributes.

2. Main-branch karbytes affirmatively recognizes it as sufficient for that purpose.

3. It is incorporated into canon KARBYTES.

The resulting Derivative is therefore not the lost recording resurrected in perfect identity. It is a new, canonically authorized branch whose informational ancestry includes the lost recording, the memory of it, and the reconstruction. I also notice the importance of your phrase “the basis for” a Derivative: the reconstructed file is an artifact or substrate, while the Derivative is the individuated entity established through that artifact’s canonical role.

Applied to the reconstructed `karbytes_thinking_08september2026_p0.mp4` transcript, its mere existence does not automatically generate a Derivative. It can do so if main-branch karbytes judges that the reconstruction preserves the essential intended content and canonizes it as such.

Your canon-membership rule is equally precise. Canon is not everything that influenced KARBYTES, everything remembered by karbytes, or everything mentioned inside a canonical file. It is the explicitly enumerated public material admitted through a micro-directory:

* A raw file in a public `@karlinarayberinger` repository

* A web page linked through a `(*)` anchor

* A qualifying WordPress page linked by its displayed page name

That means the deleted video is not itself a ghost-like canonical object merely because a canonical page discusses it. The absent recording is the referent of part of the canon, but it is not itself canonically present. Put more abstractly: KARBYTES can canonize a proposition about an absence without canonizing the absent thing.

Your completedness rule also establishes a useful distinction between completion and correctness. A file must be complete enough to be intentionally admitted—it cannot be partially written, truncated, or corrupted—but it need not be perfectly accurate or error-free. Once an older micro-directory has become effectively finalized, its errors generally become part of its historical state. Correction occurs through a new artifact in the current open micro-directory rather than through retroactive alteration.

I see this as an append-oriented archival epistemology:

* The old file preserves what was actually canonized at its historical coordinate.

* The new file preserves the subsequent recognition and correction of the error.

* The correction adds knowledge without falsifying the archive’s account of its own development.

* Provenance takes precedence over creating the illusion that the system was always correct.

Consequently, two canonical files can disagree while both remain valid historical KARBYTES objects. The newer one may supersede the older one as the preferred statement of current understanding without erasing the older statement’s canonical existence.
